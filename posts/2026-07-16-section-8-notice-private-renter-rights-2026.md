## If you get a notice to leave

From 1 May 2026, your landlord cannot end a private tenancy in England without giving a legal reason. Section 21 notices — where landlords could ask you to leave without a reason — are now abolished.

If your landlord wants you to leave, they must now serve a Section 8 notice. This notice must name the specific legal reason, called a possession ground. You get the chance to contest it in court.

This gives you much stronger protection than before.

## How long you have to stay

The notice period depends on the ground your landlord uses.

For most grounds, the law requires your landlord to give you at least 4 months' notice before they can even apply to court. This includes situations where a landlord wants to sell the property or move a family member in.

If the reason is rent arrears — known as Ground 8 — the notice period is 4 weeks. But your landlord can only use Ground 8 if you owe at least 3 months' rent. Before May 2026, the threshold was 2 months' rent. So if you have fallen behind, your landlord now has to wait longer before the notice becomes valid.

## You can challenge the notice in court

Every Section 8 case requires a court process. Your landlord cannot remove you without a judge considering the case.

If you receive a notice for rent arrears, you can still pay off what you owe before the court hearing date. If you manage to clear the debt, you can tell the court. The judge takes your circumstances into account and may decide you can stay.

If you claim Universal Credit (UC), get help as soon as possible. Citizens Advice can explain whether you can ask for a managed payment. This is where your housing costs are paid directly to your landlord, which can stop arrears building up further.

## Your unprotected deposit could protect you

When you first moved in, your landlord was legally required to put your deposit into a government-approved deposit protection scheme. They also must have given you written information about the scheme.

If they did not do this, the court cannot give your landlord a possession order to evict you — at least not for most grounds. Antisocial behaviour is one exception, but almost every other ground is blocked until the landlord sorts out the deposit.

Look back at your old tenancy paperwork. If you cannot find a scheme name or reference number, your landlord may have never protected your deposit. That matters when fighting a possession claim.

## Getting help

A notice to leave can feel overwhelming. You do not have to deal with it alone.

**Citizens Advice** offers free, confidential housing advice. Advisers can check whether your notice is valid, explain your rights, and help you respond. **Shelter** runs a free helpline and can support you through the court process if it gets that far.

Acting quickly gives you more options. The sooner you seek advice, the more time you have to find a solution.
