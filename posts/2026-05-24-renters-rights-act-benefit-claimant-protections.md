## What changed on 1 May 2026

The Renters' Rights Act came into force on 1 May 2026. It is the biggest shake-up to private renting in England in nearly 40 years.

If you are looking for a home in the private rented sector and you get Universal Credit (UC) or Housing Benefit, this law gives you stronger protections — from the moment you start your search.

## Landlords can no longer refuse you for claiming benefits

For years, many property adverts said "no DSS" — meaning the landlord would not take tenants who claimed benefits. That practice is now illegal.

From 1 May 2026, a private landlord in England cannot refuse to let you view a property, withhold information about it, or turn down your application — just because you receive benefits. The same protection applies if you have children.

Letting agents working on behalf of landlords are covered by the same rules. If you see an advert that still says "no benefits", it does not mean the landlord can legally act on it. You have the right to apply.

A landlord also cannot add a clause to your tenancy agreement that bans you from claiming benefits in future.

## You are safer once you move in

Under the old rules, a landlord could give you a Section 21 notice and ask you to leave without giving any reason at all. Section 21 "no fault" evictions are now abolished.

Your landlord must now have a specific legal reason to end your tenancy. That gives you much greater security in your home once you are settled.

## Other changes that help you

Several more rights arrived on the same date:

- **Rent rises are limited.** Your landlord can only raise your rent once a year.
- **You can challenge a big increase.** If your landlord tries to raise your rent above the going market rate, you can ask a tribunal to review it.
- **Less money upfront.** Your landlord cannot ask for more than 1 month's rent before you move in.
- **Rolling tenancies.** All private tenancies now run month to month with no fixed end date. You can leave with 2 months' notice whenever you are ready.

## What to do if a landlord still refuses you

Some landlords may not yet know the rules have changed. Others may try to find other reasons to say no.

If you think you have been refused a tenancy because you claim benefits, do not give up straight away. Free advice is available from [Citizens Advice](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/) and [Shelter](https://england.shelter.org.uk/) — both can help you understand your rights and what you can do next.
